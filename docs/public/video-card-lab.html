"Get started in 10 minutes": ten directions for the video card

The card has to say three things before anyone reads a word: this is a video, not a demo; the video builds a real invoice from nothing; and it is a well-made ten minutes, worth clicking. Every direction below keeps the shipped card chrome and copy and changes only the picture. Rest the pointer on a card to play its sequence (about nine seconds, one pass, holding the final frame); leaving the card resets it.

What staysCard surface, 48px inset, title, description, a text link with the duration. The whole card is the link. The picture is a fixed 576×324 frame (16:9 at the card's inner width) that scales down on phones, so one layout serves every width.
Video vocabulary usedPlay button, duration, a running timecode, a progress track with chapter ticks, chapter bars and thumbnails, captions, lower thirds, a "Watch the video" pill that replaces the play button while a sequence runs.
PlaceholdersDuration 9:48, chapter names and timecodes, "187 lines", the caption sentences and the poster frames are stand-ins for the published video. The invoice is the real tutorial render, cut into its content bands so it can be assembled piece by piece.

A

Split screen rest: finished frame · hover: rebuilds from an empty editor

Why it makes you clickIt is an honest preview of the video itself: editor on the left, the document on the right, moving together. A developer sees in two seconds that this is real code producing a real PDF, not slides. At rest the finished frame doubles as a dense, credible poster.
MotionThe editor empties and fourteen lines type in behind a caret. As each structural call lands, the matching part of the invoice appears: Margin brings the blank sheet, Header its band, Content the addresses and then the table rows sweeping in, Footer the page number. The play button yields to a "Watch the video" pill, the track fills under chapter ticks, the timecode runs.
Trade-offsThe busiest of the ten at thumbnail size: 11px code is readable on a desktop and decorative on a phone. It depends on crisp code typography (the quick-start window already sets that style) and on the dark frame sitting well on the light card.

B

Render sweep rest: a blueprint sheet · hover: the render travels down the page

Why it makes you clickOne large sheet, dashed boxes where content will go, a play button: the plainest possible statement that a document is about to be made. The sweep line borrows from scanners and progressive renders, which reads as work being done rather than a slideshow.
MotionA thin blue line with a soft glow travels down the page over seven seconds. Above it the finished invoice is already there; below it the wireframe waits and is taken away as the line passes. The timecode runs; at the end a small chip reports "invoice.pdf · 187 lines". The play button yields to the pill.
Trade-offsIt says nothing about code or about how the library thinks; the whole promise rests on one page. The rest state is a near-empty sheet, so it is the least rich poster of the ten, but also the calmest card on the page.

C

Chapters rest: last chapter · hover: the playhead walks six chapters

Why it makes you clickA segmented progress bar and a row of stage thumbnails are the most widely recognised "this is a video" vocabulary there is. The six thumbnails show the invoice growing from a blank page, so the from-scratch story is visible before anything moves, and the chapter names promise structure: what you will learn, in what order.
MotionThe playhead fills one segment per chapter. Each chapter lifts its thumbnail, swaps the title in the corner and builds the poster in step: header, addresses, the table sweeping in, then page two for the totals and finally both pages side by side for paging.
Trade-offsThe most player chrome of the ten, and the thumbnails are small (36px). Chapter names and timecodes must match the published video, which is upkeep but also a feature: the thumbnails can later deep-link to the chapters.

Lower thirds rest: finished frame · hover: five captions pass, the line counter climbs

Why it makes you clickBroadcast lower-third graphics say "produced, narrated video", not screen recording. The line counter in the corner makes the "under 200 lines" promise concrete and watchable: a number you can see climbing while the invoice fills in behind it.
MotionFive lower thirds slide in from the left and out to the right, one per chapter. Behind them the render assembles band by band. The counter climbs to 187 with an ease that matches the chapter cadence; the track fills and the timecode runs.
Trade-offsText overlays on a thumbnail turn into an advert if overdone, so one line of copy, one blue bar, nothing else. A light frame keeps it calmer than A, C and E, at the price of showing no code at all.

Why it makes you clickSubtitles under a picture are an unmistakable video cue, and reading one good sentence of narration sells the voice-over before a sound is played. The code window in the corner says "you will see the code", without the density of A.

Syllabus rest: blank page and the outline · hover: the playhead walks the chapters

Why it makes you clickA list of chapters with timecodes answers the manager's question ("what do I get for ten minutes?") and the developer's ("does it cover tables and paging?") at once. Confidence comes from structure, not effects: this is a course outline, and the small monitor beside it says the course is a video.
MotionA play marker walks down the list, one chapter at a time; finished chapters get a check and settle into the body colour. The page in the monitor builds in step, so the outline and the result are read together.
Trade-offsThe least cinematic of the ten: the weakest pull to click, the strongest sense of knowing what you would get. The chapter titles must stay short to survive the phone width, where the list and the monitor shrink together.

Assembly rest: exploded parts with their API names · hover: they snap into a page

Why it makes you clickIt shows the library's mental model, a page assembled from a header, content and a footer, which is exactly what the video teaches. Exploded parts labelled with the calls that make them invite curiosity, and watching them snap into place is quietly satisfying.
MotionThe four parts fly to their place in reading order, their labels fade, the dashed target disappears and the finished page flashes once. The track and timecode run for a shorter sequence (four and a half seconds), since there is nothing left to watch after the snap.
Trade-offsThe weakest video signal: the play button and the track carry it alone, and the card could be read as an interactive demo. The exploded rest state is less tidy than a poster, and the smallest part (the footer) is a sliver.

Code to layout rest: finished frame · hover: each call lights up and points at its region

Why it makes you clickThe "aha" of QuestPDF is that every fluent call is a region on the page. Drawing a line from the call to its box teaches that in one glance and makes the longer explanation worth ten minutes. Where A shows the video, H shows the idea behind it.
MotionEight lines highlight in turn. For each, a connector draws from the code to the page, the region's outline appears, and the content of that region fades in. Nothing types, so the frame is calmer than A.
Trade-offsConnectors are a diagram idiom and can read as documentation rather than a video, so the play button and track matter more here. Still a dark, dense frame, and the logo connector has to route around the page.

Poster rest: title card with a pulsing play pill · hover: the pages turn to face you

Why it makes you clickA cinematic title card, the way product launch films are presented: it frames the tutorial as a flagship piece, not a screencast. The pages standing at an angle in a blue glow give depth, and a white pill with the duration is the classic invitation.
MotionAt rest a faint ring pulses out of the play pill. On hover the two pages turn towards the viewer and a sheen passes over the front one, the topic chips light up in order (Pages, Layout, Tables, Paging), the ring quickens and the track fills.
Trade-offsThe most "marketing" of the ten: it shows the result, not the making, so the from-scratch story lives in the words. The headline inside the frame must differ from the card title, and it only works with careful type; done carelessly it is a stock thumbnail.

Blueprint rest: a technical drawing of the page · hover: it fills with the real render

Why it makes you clickA drawing of the page with its margins, bands and dimensions, turning into the real document, says "engineered, understood, precise". It speaks to the senior developer and the architect, and it is the most mature-looking of the ten without a single effect for its own sake.
MotionThe sheet turns white, then each band fills from the top: header, addresses, the table sweeping in, the footer. Every label on the rail is checked off as its band lands, the blue ink and the grid fade, and the backdrop settles to a plain studio grey around a finished render.
Trade-offsThe quietest video signal, carried by the play button, the timecode and the track. The blueprint look is a step away from the homepage's soft surfaces, so it must stay restrained: one ink colour, hairlines, no glow.

Reading the table Most visitors never hover, so the rest state is the card. Designs whose rest state is a complete, finished frame (A, C, D, E, H, I) start ahead; B, F, G and J show a beginning and rely on the play button to carry the "video" message. The gallery card beside it is a row of finished pages, so a video card that is also "finished pages" (I, and C's rest state) reads as two versions of one idea, while a code-and-page frame (A, E, H) or a drawing (J) keeps the pair distinct.
Recommendation: A, with two borrowed parts. The split-screen frame is the only direction that is literally a frame of the video, so it cannot over-promise, and it carries all three messages at rest: a play button on a 16:9 frame, code beside the page it produces, a finished invoice. Borrow the segmented chapter bar from C in place of the plain track (it is the strongest single "video" cue and costs one row of pixels), and the single caption line from E under the frame while the sequence plays, which previews the narration without E's text density. Runner-up: C, if the published video has clean chapters and we want the card to deep-link into them later. If the homepage should stay quieter, J is the mature alternative: it trades some video signal for the most confident picture of the ten.
Before it ships Replace the stand-ins with the video's own poster frame, duration and chapter list; cut the typed snippet to the lines the video actually types first; measure the card height against the gallery card (the 16:9 frame is 324px tall where the shipped page stack is 208px, so the gallery card's visual grows or the frame shrinks to about 260px); check light, dark and 390px; and delete this file.
